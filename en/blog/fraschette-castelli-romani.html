The fraschette — the tavern of the Castelli.

November 20266 minute readEditorial team

A leafy branch hung over the door, a few barrels, wooden benches and a jug of this year's wine. For centuries nothing more was needed in the Castelli Romani to make a feast. November, the month of new wine, is the right time to understand what a fraschetta really is, and how to behave once you sit down.

A branch over the door

A fraschetta is a popular venue devoted to pouring new wine, possibly with simple food, found mainly in the Castelli Romani. The name probably comes from the medieval custom, shared by several parts of Italy, of hanging a leafy branch, often laurel, over the door of houses where new wine could be drunk for a fee: a real sign, which let winegrowers recoup the costs of the harvest (Wikipedia). Another theory links the word to Frascata, the old name of Frascati, but it is considered less likely (Wikipedia).

Unlike the osteria, run for years by an innkeeper, a fraschetta could start on the initiative of a small producer who sold his own wine for a limited time (Wikipedia). Between the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the best-known were in Frascati, Grottaferrata, Marino and Albano; today their undisputed kingdom is the historic centre of Ariccia (Wikipedia).

The age of the fagottari

The furnishings were spare: barrels lined up along one side, benches and rough tables, a few winemaking tools on the walls, the cellar dug in at the back. There was no kitchen: besides wine there was at most bread and hard-boiled eggs, to prepare the palate. Everything else came from home, often in hemp bundles, hence the nickname, first neutral and then gently ironic, of fagottari (Wikipedia). Soon stalls selling something to eat with the wine appeared next to the fraschette, and among them porchetta (Wikipedia).

The tradition of the Castelli Romani

What you eat today

Only in modern times did it become possible to have a full meal in a fraschetta, and many now have a real kitchen (Wikipedia). The ritual has survived, though: you start with cured meats, fresh and aged cheeses, olives and pickles, in portions so generous that the pasta course often becomes optional and a main course a rarity. If you get to the pasta, it is Roman: carbonara, amatriciana, arrabbiata (Wikipedia). At the centre of the table sits Ariccia's porchetta, a PGI product since 14 June 2011, made by boning the pig and seasoning it with rosemary, black pepper and garlic (Wikipedia).

How you drink

The wine is served loose, in carafes with old names: the two-litre boccale, also called Barzilai after the late nineteenth-century Roman politician who offered wine to his voters, the one-litre mezzo boccale or tubbo, the half-litre fojetta and the quartino (Wikipedia). The typical wine is Romanella, a lightly sparkling red (Wikipedia). Tradition links its name to Giovanna Rinaldi, housekeeper of Cardinal Ferdinando Taverna, nicknamed Romanella (La Cucina Italiana). Alongside it you find the wines of the Colli Albani, covered in our guide to Castelli wine.

The seasons of the fraschetta

The fraschetta was born tied to the vineyard calendar: it was where the wine of that year's vintage was sold and tasted on the spot (Wikipedia). That is why November, when the new wine is ready, keeps something of its original meaning. The other big date is in September, with Ariccia's Porchetta Festival, founded in 1950 and held every year in that month (Wikipedia). The fraschette stay busy all year round, with locals as well as Romans and tourists (Wikipedia).

Practical tips

From Castel Gandolfo the nearest fraschette are in Ariccia, just past Albano. Many places offer set menus with starters, a pasta course, wine and water: as a guide, a 25-euro set menu at a fraschetta in the centre (TheFork). Prices vary from place to place, so it is worth asking before ordering. In some places you can still enjoy old-style hospitality (Wikipedia): if you want to bring something from home, though, always ask the host first. For a route around the Castelli, Visit Lazio suggests a tour from fraschetta to fraschetta (Visit Lazio).
