Villa Cybo — the bridge that was never built.

November 20266 minute readEditorial team

Between the Apostolic Palace and the gardens of Villa Barberini lies a third, lesser-known core of the Pontifical Villas: Villa Cybo. It is the story of a cardinal who loved gardens, of a public road that cut his villa in two, and of a bridge that was designed and never built. The link would only come two centuries later, and in a different form.

An architect, then a cardinal

The house began as the private home of the architect Francesco Fontana, appointed surveyor of the Papal Villa by Clement XI in 1706, who decided to build himself a villa next to the popes' own (Wikipedia). In 1717 Cardinal Camillo Cybo, then Auditor of the Apostolic Chamber, had it handed over to him “as his noble home and villa” (Vatican City Governorate). Born in Massa in 1681, son of Duke Carlo II Cybo-Malaspina and Teresa Pamphilj, Cybo became prefect of the Apostolic Palace in 1725 and a cardinal in 1729; he died in Rome in 1743 (Wikipedia). As majordomo of the Sacred Palaces he was also the first governor of Castel Gandolfo (Wikipedia).

In front of the house the cardinal bought a plot of about three hectares, bordering the village above and, below, towards the sea, the road known as the “Galleria di sotto”, and turned it into a garden full of marble, statues and fountains (Vatican City Governorate). According to the account reported by ACI Stampa, many of the sculptures came from the family estates in Massa, and the main garden, terraced on the slope below the village, ended in a two-level Italian parterre (ACI Stampa). A costly luxury: the guide to the cardinal's archive in the State Archive of Rome lists the cost of building the villa among the expenses that strained his finances (State Archive of Rome).

The flaw: a road in the middle

The villa, however, had “a serious flaw: the house and the garden were separated by the public road”, that is, the Galleria di sotto. The cardinal planned to join them with a bridge at the level of the garden's main floor, but “the project was never carried out; we do not know whether for lack of time or of money” (Vatican City Governorate). The Directorate of the Pontifical Villas confirms it today: the planned bridge was never built (Pontifical Villas). Wikipedia adds that Fontana's own design already foresaw a gallery bridge linking the house to the Papal Palace, which also stayed on paper (Wikipedia).

To the popes, for 18,000 scudi

After the cardinal's death his heirs sold the villa to the Duke of Bracciano, Livio Odescalchi. In March 1773 Clement XIV, looking for a better place to walk than Urban VIII's small garden, bought it on the same terms: 18,000 scudi (Vatican City Governorate). In 1841 Gregory XVI entrusted the house to the Brothers of the Christian Schools, who opened a boys' school there (Wikipedia). In between, the garden went through hard times: according to ACI Stampa, Pius VI moved the finest statues to the Quirinal, and the estate, leased out in perpetuity, ended up growing 22,000 artichoke plants (ACI Stampa).

The link comes after 1929

With the Lateran Pacts of 1929 the Pontifical Villas grew to include Villa Barberini, and the works that followed finally connected the three cores, the Garden of the Moor, Villa Cybo and Villa Barberini: a bridge between the Barberini estate and Villa Cybo, and a loggia leading from Villa Cybo to the Palace over the public road, on the arch of the old Roman Gate (Vatican City Governorate). So this is not the bridge Cybo dreamed of between house and garden, but a different passage, built to join the villas. The sources disagree on one detail: according to Wikipedia, the viaduct towards Villa Barberini, known as the “Colonnato”, already dates from 1773 and was only faced with peperino stone under Pius XI (Wikipedia).

Villa Cybo today

In the garden of Villa Cybo, Pius XII commissioned the Audience Hall in 1957, designed by Enrico Galeazzi and opened by John XXIII in 1959; John Paul II entrusted it to the Focolare Movement, which uses it as the Centro Mariapoli for conferences (Wikipedia). The gardens are reached from the Garden of the Moor, the oldest core (ACI Stampa). The Pontifical Villas entrance given by the Vatican Museums is precisely that of Villa Cybo, on Via Massimo D'Azeglio, ten to fifteen minutes' walk from the station (Vatican Museums).
