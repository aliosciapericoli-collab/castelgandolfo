Castel Gandolfo in November — the village without tourists.

October 20265 minute readEditorial team

Once the summer crowds and autumn festivals have gone, Castel Gandolfo becomes again what it was before the papal holidays: a small village on the rim of a crater. November is the month to see it that way, with the lake veiled in mist, quiet squares and the Pope's museums open without queues.

The lake in the mist

The water lies at 293 metres above sea level, at the bottom of the crater, and reaches a depth of 168 metres. Its climate is very humid and noticeably colder than the village above, because of temperature inversion: on cold mornings mist lingers over the water and clears only with the sun (Wikipedia). From the belvedere in the old town the view is of a white bowl from which the woods of the far shore slowly emerge.

When the mist lifts you can walk down to the shore. The lakeside road runs along the whole northern shore, past the facilities built for the rowing and canoe events of the 1960 Rome Olympics; on the eastern shore a woodland path passes the remains of a Roman villa. The lake's perimeter is about 10 kilometres (Wikipedia). In November bring waterproof shoes: the eastern shore is the rainiest.

The Pope's Tuesday

Since September 2025 Leo XIV has made a habit of spending his weekly day of rest at Castel Gandolfo, usually arriving on Monday evening (La Croix). On a November evening, 18 November 2025, as he left Villa Barberini, he told journalists how he spends those hours: a little reading, a little work, the daily correspondence, a little tennis and a little swimming (EWTN News). The reporters had waited for hours on the pavement despite the cold and the mist rising from the lake (Religion Digital / EFE). The residence stays private and the papal agenda can change, but on Tuesday evenings the square keeps an atmosphere of its own.

“A break during the week that helps a lot.”
Leo XIV at Castel Gandolfo, 18 November 2025

Palace and gardens off season

The Apostolic Palace and gardens are managed by the Vatican Museums and can be visited only by booking (Papal Villas). In the low season opening hours change from day to day: the reference is the official 2026-2027 calendar of the Papal Palace, which also marks closure days and notes that visitors are asked to leave the rooms half an hour before closing (Vatican Museums). 1 November, All Saints' Day, is among the Vatican Museums' closure days (Vatican Museums): check before you set off.

The advantage of November is calm. The rooms overlooking the lake and the Giardino del Moro can be crossed without large groups, with the low afternoon light coming through the windows. The route also includes the permanent exhibition “Castel Gandolfo 1944” (Papal Villas), devoted to the months when the Villas sheltered thousands of displaced people: a story that November's silence makes even more intense. The Borgo Laudato Si', with its olive groves, also takes visits by booking: hours and availability should be checked on its official site (Borgo Laudato Si').

New wine and fraschette

November is also the month of new wine. In Italy vino novello may be sold from 30 October of the vintage year (Wikipedia), and in the Castelli Romani the new wine has a traditional home: the fraschetta. The name comes from the custom of hanging a leafy branch, a frasca, over the door of houses where new wine could be drunk, with customers bringing their own food; today the heart of the phenomenon is nearby Ariccia, where some streets and a square are dominated by these taverns, paired with porchetta (Wikipedia).

A November day can therefore look like this: the morning on the belvedere watching the mist dissolve, the Palace visit at midday, a walk along the lake and in the evening a glass of Alban Hills wine in the fraschette of Ariccia.
