History · Papal Villas

Spring 1626 — the first Pope on holiday.

October 20267 minute readEditorial team

On 10 May 1626 a papal procession left Rome for the Alban Hills. At its head was Urban VIII Barberini, who that day became the first Pope to spend his holidays in the palace of Castel Gandolfo. Since then, with long pauses and returns, the lakeside town has remained the summer home of the popes: this year it celebrated four centuries.

A cardinal in love with the lake

Even before his election, Cardinal Maffeo Barberini had chosen Castel Gandolfo for his holidays, drawn by its incomparable panoramic position and convinced it was the healthiest place in the Castelli Romani. He had a modest dwelling built near the walls, on the upper floor of the tower that still rises above the Porta Romana; his stables outside the walls also survive (Vatican City State).

The town had only recently passed to the Church. In July 1596, under Clement VIII, the Apostolic Camera took possession of Castel Gandolfo from the Savelli, who had failed to pay a debt of 150,000 scudi; on 27 May 1604 a consistorial decree incorporated it permanently into the Church's temporal dominion (Vatican City State). Yet no Pope had spent a holiday there.

Holidays in a home of their own

Elected in the summer of 1623 as Urban VIII, Barberini did not invent papal holidays: for two years he spent them at Frascati as the guest of Cardinal Scipione Borghese. He did, however, want the pontiff to have a summer residence of his own. His biographer Andrea Nicoletti records his aim of ensuring that popes could holiday in their own palaces, since it did not seem fitting to make use of other people's houses (Vatican City State).

So he decided to adapt the old fortress of the Gandolfi and the Savelli. The works, carried out between 1624 and 1626 (Vatican News), were entrusted to Carlo Maderno, with Bartolomeo Breccioli and Domenico Castelli as assistant architects. The fortress was absorbed into the palace; the wing facing the lake and the left part of the present facade, up to the main door, were built, and the Giardino del Moro was laid out, small and still faithful to its first design, with regular beds edged in myrtle. The Florentine Simone Lagi frescoed the private chapel, the oratory and the sacristy (Vatican City State).

10 May 1626

With the works complete, Urban VIII set 10 May 1626 as the departure date for his first holiday at Castel Gandolfo (Papal Villas), a date also given by (Vatican News). A local tradition instead recalls a solemn entry in October, announced at dawn by cannon fire and escorted by a procession of carriages (Vatican News): the two memories can coexist, since the Pope came twice a year.

After 1626 Urban VIII returned faithfully to the villa for another eleven years, in April or more often May and again in October, for stays of two to three weeks. His days were methodical: in his leisure hours he never lacked the company of men of letters and scholars, he loved walking and, in the early years, long rides through the woods. So that government would not stall, he received ministers and ambassadors as usual. In 1637 an illness made people fear for his life, and from then on he gave up the villa, convinced with his doctors that the heavier air of Rome suited him better (Vatican City State).

Four centuries, with some pauses

The tradition was not unbroken. His successor Innocent X never came to Castel Gandolfo; Alexander VII Chigi returned twice a year and called on Bernini to complete the palace; then, apart from a single night in 1697, no Pope holidayed there for 44 years (Vatican City State). From 1870 to 1929 the popes did not leave the Vatican, and only Pius XI resumed using the residence, from 1934 (Wikipedia).

The bond, however, endured. On 5 and 6 September 2026 Castel Gandolfo celebrated 400 years of papal presence with a Mass for its patron Saint Sebastian and a historical procession in seventeenth-century costume, some thirty performers recreating Urban VIII's arrival by carriage. The celebration was organised by the municipality, the Pontifical Parish and local historian Luciano Mariani (Vatican News).

Visitors to the Apostolic Palace today can still step into Urban VIII's small chapel (Vatican News): the starting point of a story four hundred years long.
